Seasonal Cattle Nutrition & Herd Protection

Each season presents distinct physiological challenges for ruminants. Follow our seasonal feeding and prevention guidelines.

Spring Pasture & Grass Tetany

Lush early spring grasses are rich in potassium and nitrogen but critically low in magnesium and crude fiber.

  • Provide high-magnesium mineral blocks.
  • Buffer lush pasture with long-stem dry hay.
  • Watch for frothy pasture bloat.

Summer Heat Stress Mitigation

When the Temperature-Humidity Index (THI) exceeds 68, cows drop dry matter intake by up to 25%, risking severe acidosis.

  • Shift 60% of feeding to evening/night.
  • Boost dietary potassium (1.5%) & sodium.
  • Feed bypass fat to maintain energy density.

Autumn Silage & Weaning

Transitioning from fresh grazing to fermented silage requires strict bunker compaction to prevent aerobic spoilage.

  • Inoculate corn silage with homofermentative strains.
  • Administer pre-weaning calf respiratory vaccines.
  • Step up booster rations for replacement heifers.

Winter Energy Maintenance

For every degree below lower critical temperature (32°F / 0°C), a cow requires a 1% increase in Total Digestible Nutrients (TDN).

  • Increase concentrate energy by 10-15%.
  • Ensure continuous warm, non-freezing water.
  • Maintain dry bedding to protect udder health.

Preventative Medicine

Annual Herd Vaccination & Parasite Control

Vaccination protocols should always be executed under the supervision of a licensed herd veterinarian. Preventative immunization saves up to $180 per head annually in treatment and culled cows.

Bovine Respiratory Disease (BRD) Complex

Protects against IBR, BVD (Types 1 & 2), PI3, and BRSV. Administer modified-live vaccine (MLV) to heifers 30 days prior to breeding.

Clostridial 7-Way & 8-Way (Blackleg)

Essential immunization against spore-forming soil bacteria. Initial dose at 2 months of age, booster at weaning, then annual booster.

Strategic Fluke & Roundworm Drenching

Drench with BoviDeworm broad-spectrum anthelmintic suspension at turnout in spring and housing in late autumn.

Veterinary Guidance

Frequently Asked Livestock Care Questions

Expert answers to common feeding and metabolic health challenges faced on modern dairy operations.

How can I tell if my cows are suffering from Subacute Ruminal Acidosis (SARA)?

Subacute Ruminal Acidosis occurs when rumen pH drops between 5.2 and 5.6 for prolonged periods. Classic symptoms include reduced rumination (less than 50% of resting cows chewing their cud), bubbly diarrhea with undigested grain particles, fluctuating daily milk yields, and unexplained drop in milk butterfat percentage below 3.2%. Administering sustained-release sodium bicarbonate boluses and balancing effective fiber (peNDF) immediately restores rumen balance.

Why are organic chelated minerals superior to generic inorganic minerals?

Inorganic trace minerals (such as oxides and sulfates) dissociate readily in the rumen and bind with antagonists like sulfur, molybdenum, and iron, rendering them unabsorbable in the small intestine. In contrast, organic chelated minerals (such as BoviMax zinc and copper glycinates) remain chemically intact through the acidic abomasum and are actively absorbed through amino acid transport pathways, achieving 3x higher bioavailability.

What is the target blood Brix percentage for newborn calf colostrum?

Using an optical or digital Brix refractometer, maternal colostrum should test at 22% Brix or higher (equivalent to 50 g/L of IgG antibodies) before feeding. Newborn calves should receive 4 liters of high-quality colostrum within the first 2 hours of life, followed by a second feeding of 2 liters at 12 hours. At 24 to 48 hours of life, a serum Brix reading of 8.4% or higher indicates successful passive transfer of immunity.

How does bypass fat increase milk production without triggering rumen upset?

Free unsaturated oils (like vegetable oils) coat fiber particles in the rumen, suffocating fiber-digesting microbes and depressing butterfat. Fractionated bypass fats (calcium salts of fatty acids or high-palmitic prilled fats) are insoluble at normal ruminal pH (6.5). They pass completely inert through the rumen into the acidic abomasum (pH 2.5), where they dissociate and are digested in the small intestine, providing dense energy without hindering rumen bacteria.
